Phase 0 · Orientation

Understand the AI Engineer Role

Before writing a line of code, know what job you're actually training for. "AI Engineer" gets used loosely — here's where it sits relative to the roles it's usually confused with.

What an AI Engineer actually does

In one lineBuilds and ships products powered by ML/LLM models — the plumbing, evaluation, and reliability around a model, not usually the model architecture research itself.
Typical dayWiring an LLM into an API, designing a retrieval pipeline, writing evals, debugging why an agent looped, watching latency/cost dashboards, reviewing a teammate's prompt-injection fix.
Core loopPrototype fast with an LLM API → evaluate rigorously → productionize (API, monitoring, cost control) → iterate on real usage data.

Types of AI Engineer roles you'll see in job posts

  • LLM/GenAI Engineer — builds LLM-powered products: RAG, agents, copilots.
  • Applied ML Engineer — ships classical + deep learning models into products.

Phase 2 · Foundations

Mathematics for AI

You do not need a pure-math degree's worth of theory. You need enough intuition to know why a model is misbehaving. Every topic below is tied back to an ML/DL concept — skip the ones that don't connect to something you're about to build.

AreaKey topicsDirect connection to ML/DL
Linear Algebra 🟢Vectors, matrices, dot product, matrix multiplication, transpose, norms, eigenvalues/eigenvectors, vector spacesEvery layer in a neural net is a matrix multiply; embeddings are vectors; PCA runs on eigenvectors.
Probability 🟢Conditional probability, Bayes' theorem, random variables, distributions, expectation, variance, independenceSoftmax outputs are probability distributions; Naive Bayes and language model sampling both run on this directly.
Statistics 🟢Mean, median, variance, std-dev, correlation, covariance, sampling, hypothesis testing, confidence intervalsFeature scaling, A/B testing model changes, understanding whether a metric improvement is real or noise.
Calculus 🟢Derivatives, partial derivatives, gradients, chain ruleBackpropagation is the chain rule applied millions of times.
Optimization 🟢Loss functions, gradient descent, learning rate, local/global minima, regularizationThis is literally how every model you'll ever train "learns."
Embeddings intuition (from linear algebra)Vector spaces, cosine similarityFoundation for Phase 8 (embeddings) and Phase 9 (RAG).
Depth guide: aim to compute a gradient by hand for a 2-layer network once, and understand cosine similarity intuitively. You do not need measure theory, real analysis, or proofs of convergence to be a productive AI Engineer.

  • Probability essentials 🟢
  • Statistics essentials 🟢
  • Calculus & gradients 🟢

Phase 6 · Core ML

Transformers & Modern AI

Every LLM you'll ever call is a transformer. This is the single highest-leverage architecture to understand deeply in 2026.

Self-attention, unpacked

Query/Key/ValueEvery token asks "what am I looking for" (Q), advertises "what do I contain" (K), and carries "what I'll pass on" (V).
Multi-head attentionSeveral attention computations run in parallel, each free to learn a different kind of relationship (syntax, coreference, etc.).
Positional encodingAttention has no built-in sense of order — position info is injected explicitly.
Encoder / DecoderEncoder sees the whole input at once (good for understanding); decoder generates one token at a time, only looking backward (good for generation).
Model familyTypeBest atExamples
Encoder-onlyBidirectional understandingClassification, embeddings, searchBERT and its descendants
Decoder-onlyAutoregressive generationChat, completion, reasoningThe GPT-style family — most modern LLMs
Encoder-decoderSequence-to-sequenceTranslation, summarizationT5-style models
Note: almost every LLM you'll interact with via API in 2026 is decoder-only. Understand encoder models for embeddings/reranking (Phase 8) — but don't over-invest in encoder-decoder architectures unless you specialize in translation/summarization.
  • Self-attention & multi-head attention 🟢
  • Positional encoding 🟢
  • Encoder vs decoder vs encoder-decoder 🟢
  • BERT / GPT / T5 family differences 🟢

Phase 8 · Modern AI

Embeddings & Vector Databases

Embeddings turn meaning into geometry — everything RAG and semantic search does builds on this idea.

What are embeddingsDense vectors where semantic similarity ≈ geometric closeness — an embedding model turns "a happy dog" and "a joyful puppy" into nearby points.
Cosine similarityThe default distance metric for comparing embedding vectors, since it ignores magnitude and only measures direction.
Vector DBWhen to reach for it
FAISSLocal, in-memory, no server — great for prototypes and research.
ChromaSimple embedded DB for small-to-medium apps and quick RAG prototypes.
QdrantProduction-grade, self-hostable, strong filtering support.
PineconeFully managed cloud vector DB — fast to ship, less infra ownership.
WeaviateManaged or self-hosted, built-in hybrid search and modules.
pgvector (Postgres)When you'd rather not add a new database to your stack at all.
  • How embeddings are generated & compared 🟢
  • Set up at least one vector DB end-to-end 🟢

Phase 10 · Modern AI

AI Agents

Not every LLM app is an "agent." Know the difference before you build one — most production systems in 2026 are still simple workflows, not open-ended agents.

System typeWhat it means
ChatbotConversational LLM, no tools, no state beyond chat history.
LLM applicationSingle LLM call embedded in a larger deterministic app.
WorkflowA fixed sequence of LLM/tool steps — predictable, easy to debug.
AgentThe LLM decides which tools to call and in what order, in a loop, to reach a goal.
Multi-agent systemMultiple specialized agents collaborate/hand off work to each other.
Tool/function callingPlanningMemoryState ReflectionRoutingAgent loopsHuman-in-the-loopGuardrails

Framework ecosystem — and when NOT to use one

LangGraphGraph-based orchestration for stateful, controllable agent workflows.
LangChainBroad toolkit of integrations — useful for gluing pieces together fast.
LlamaIndexStrong for data-indexing-heavy and RAG-centric agent apps.
Raw provider SDKsOften the better choice for a small, well-defined agent — fewer abstractions to debug, full control over the loop. Frameworks are a productivity tool, not a requirement.

Phase 18 · The Fork

Pick a specialization

Phases 0–17 are the shared trunk. Once you're comfortable there, the route forks — pick one track to go deep on for your first job, and treat the rest as things you'll pick up later.

LLM Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 6, 7, 9. Tools: provider SDKs, LangGraph. Projects: production RAG, evaluation harness. Does: builds and hardens LLM-powered product features.

Generative AI Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 5–7. Tools: diffusion/gen-media APIs, PyTorch. Projects: content-generation pipeline. Does: builds text/image/audio generation features.

Computer Vision Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 5 (CNNs deep). Tools: OpenCV, torchvision. Projects: detection/segmentation pipeline. Does: image/video understanding systems.

NLP Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 6. Tools: Hugging Face, spaCy. Projects: classification/NER pipeline. Does: text understanding & extraction systems.

Speech/Voice AI Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 5–7. Tools: ASR/TTS APIs, streaming audio. Projects: voice assistant. Does: real-time speech pipelines.

Recommendation Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 4 (deep). Tools: matrix factorization, embeddings, feature stores. Projects: recommender system. Does: personalization & ranking systems.

Multimodal AI Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 6, 9. Tools: vision-language models. Projects: document intelligence system. Does: systems reasoning over text + images (+ audio).

AI Research Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 2, 5, 6 (deep). Tools: PyTorch, paper-reading habit. Projects: reproduce a paper. Does: implements & evaluates novel methods.

Agent Engineer

Prereqs: Phase 9, 10. Tools: LangGraph, evaluation tooling. Projects: multi-agent system. Does: builds autonomous, tool-using systems.

Avoid The Trap

Things beginners should NOT waste time on

The AI ecosystem is enormous and moves fast — trying to keep up with all of it is how people burn out before ever shipping anything.

Learning 10 programming languages

Python takes you further in AI than fluency in five languages ever will. Depth beats breadth here.

Mastering every cloud provider

Pick one (Phase 14), go deep, and only skim the others.

Learning every AI framework

LangChain, LlamaIndex, LangGraph, CrewAI, and more launch constantly — pick one, understand the concepts, transfer them later.

Collecting certificates

A stack of course-completion certificates rarely moves a hiring decision on its own — see the section below.

Watching endless tutorials

Passive watching feels like progress and isn't. Cap tutorial time and spend the rest building.

Building only notebooks

A notebook that only you can run isn't a portfolio piece — ship it as a script, API, or app.

Copy-pasting projects

If you can't explain every architectural decision in an interview, the project won't hold up under questioning.

Chasing every new framework release

New tools launch weekly. Fundamentals (Phases 1–10) transfer across all of them; chasing hype does not.

Training giant LLMs from scratch

Unnecessary for almost every real job. Fine-tuning (LoRA/QLoRA) and RAG solve the vast majority of practical problems.

Honest Take

Certifications: what actually helps

Certifications are supporting evidence, not the main proof. A cloud certification (e.g. AWS Certified AI Practitioner / ML Specialty) can help pass an initial resume screen or show baseline cloud literacy, especially if you have no work experience. Vendor "prompt engineering" or "intro to AI" certificates from short courses carry very little weight on their own.

Why projects matter more: for most AI Engineering roles, hiring managers care whether you can debug a retrieval pipeline at 11pm, not whether you hold a badge. A working, well-documented project demonstrates judgment a certificate can't.

How to balance them: treat one relevant cloud certification as optional supporting evidence once your portfolio is solid — never as a substitute for it, and never at the cost of project time.

Read The Originals

Papers every AI Engineer should know

You don't need to memorize equations — you need to recognize the idea each paper introduced and why it mattered.

Attention Is All You Need Med

Introduced the transformer architecture itself — the single most important paper on this list. Understand self-attention, not the exact hyperparameters.

Word2Vec Easy

Showed that word meaning could be captured as vector arithmetic — the conceptual root of all modern embeddings.

BERT Med

Introduced bidirectional pretraining for language understanding — understand masked-language-modeling, skip exact training details.

GPT (foundational language modeling work) Med

Established decoder-only, autoregressive pretraining at scale as the dominant recipe for modern LLMs.

ResNet Easy

Introduced residual connections, solving the vanishing-gradient problem in deep networks — the idea still underlies transformer blocks today.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) Med

Formalized combining retrieval with generation — the direct ancestor of everything in Phase 9. Understand the motivation, not the exact benchmark numbers.

LoRA Med

Showed you can fine-tune huge models by training small low-rank adapter matrices — what makes Phase-19-style fine-tuning affordable.

RLHF-related foundational work Hard

Introduced using human preference data to align model behavior — the idea behind why modern chat models "feel" helpful and safe. Understand the concept, not the RL math.
