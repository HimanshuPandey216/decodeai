01Part I · Foundations

What is a Transformer?

A Transformer is a neural network architecture, introduced in 2017, that reads a whole sequence at once and figures out, mathematically, which parts of that sequence matter to each other. It is the architecture behind BERT, GPT, T5, and — in modified form — almost every modern large language model.

Before you touch any formula, hold on to one plain-English idea: a Transformer's core skill is deciding what to pay attention to. Give it the sentence "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big," and somewhere inside the network, the word "it" ends up linked mathematically to "trophy," not "suitcase." Every other piece of the architecture — tokenizing text, building vectors, stacking layers — exists to make that one skill (called attention) trainable at scale.

💡
Analogy first. Imagine reading a mystery novel while highlighting, for every pronoun, which earlier character it refers to. A Transformer does something similar for every single word, simultaneously, for every word in the sentence — not just pronouns. That "everyone looks at everyone" step is what makes it fundamentally different from reading left-to-right one word at a time.

The problem it solves

Language is sequential, but meaning is not local. "It" in the sentence above depends on a word eight positions away. A model that only looks at nearby words, or that has to pass information hand-to-hand through many steps, tends to lose or distort that dependency the further apart the two words are. Transformers solve this by giving every token a direct mathematical path to every other token, regardless of distance — no relay race required.

They also solve a very practical, unglamorous problem: training speed. Earlier sequence models had to process token 1, then token 2, then token 3 — one at a time, in order — because each step depended on the previous one's output. Transformers process all tokens in a sequence in parallel during training, which is a large part of why we can now train models on trillions of words. Sections 02 and 03 unpack both of these in detail.

02Part I · Foundations

Why Transformers Were Needed

Two very different problems pushed the field toward a new architecture: models kept losing track of information over long sentences, and training them took an impractically long time as datasets grew. Transformers were designed to fix both at once.

Problem 1 — sequence processing is hard because meaning isn't local

Take: "The city council refused the demonstrators a permit because they feared violence." Does "they" refer to the council or the demonstrators? A human resolves this instantly using world knowledge and sentence structure — but a model has to somehow connect "they" back to a specific noun phrase several words earlier, from among several candidates. The further apart two related words are, the harder this gets for architectures that pass information step-by-step.

Problem 2 — training was slow because processing was sequential

Before Transformers, the dominant approach (recurrent networks, covered next in Section 03) read a sentence one token at a time, carrying forward a running summary called a hidden state. Token 5 could not be processed until token 4 was done. That's fine for a short sentence, but it means you cannot use extra parallel compute (GPUs are built for parallel work) to speed up processing a single sequence — and at internet scale, that becomes the bottleneck.

What attention changed

Attention (fully introduced in Section 09) gives every token a direct, one-hop mathematical connection to every other token in the sequence — no relay race, no fixed-size bottleneck vector that has to hold "everything so far." Two consequences follow directly:

  • Long-range dependencies become just as easy as short-range ones. The path length between any two tokens is O(1) — one attention computation — regardless of how far apart they are.
  • All tokens can be processed simultaneously. Since a token's attention computation depends on the whole input at once rather than on the previous step's output, the entire sequence can be computed in parallel on a GPU during training.
Why this mattered for LLMs specifically

Large language models are trained on trillions of tokens. A architecture that must process tokens one at a time, with an information bottleneck, simply does not scale to that regime in practical time. Parallel training plus strong long-range dependency modeling is precisely the combination that made training today's large models feasible.

03Part I · Foundations

Before Transformers: RNNs and LSTMs

To appreciate what Transformers changed, you need a working picture of what came before: Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and their more capable variant, LSTMs (Long Short-Term Memory networks).

How an RNN processes a sentence

An RNN reads tokens one at a time, left to right. At each step it takes the current token's embedding and the hidden state left over from the previous step, and produces a new hidden state:

ht = f( W·xt + U·ht-1 + b )
xt
the embedding of the token at position t
ht-1
the hidden state carried over from the previous position — the model's entire "memory" of everything before position t
W, U, b
learned weight matrices and bias, shared across every time step
f
a non-linear activation (traditionally tanh)

Notice the dependency: you cannot compute ht until you have ht-1. That single line is the root of both RNN weaknesses below.

Weakness 1 — vanishing (and exploding) gradients

During training, error signals are propagated backward through every one of those steps (see Section 25). Each step multiplies the gradient by roughly the same weight matrix again and again. If that repeated multiplication shrinks the signal, gradients for early tokens vanish to near zero and the model effectively stops learning from long-range context. If it grows instead, gradients explode into instability. LSTMs (and GRUs) were invented specifically to fight this with learned "gates" that let information skip steps more cleanly — a real improvement, but a patch on the underlying sequential design, not a removal of it.

Weakness 2 — a fixed-size bottleneck

Everything the model has read so far, however long, gets compressed into one hidden-state vector of fixed size. For a 4-word sentence that's rarely a problem; for a 400-word document, that vector is being asked to do an enormous amount of lossy compression.

Weakness 3 — no parallelism across the sequence

Because step t needs the output of step t−1, you cannot compute all positions of one sequence at the same time, no matter how much parallel hardware you have. You can parallelize across different sequences in a batch, but not within one long sequence.

Don't walk away thinking "Transformers are strictly better in every way." They trade an O(n) memory/compute profile for an O(n²) one in exchange for parallelism and shorter dependency paths. For very long sequences (tens of thousands of tokens and up) that trade-off needs real engineering — which is exactly why techniques like KV caching, sparse/linear attention, and sliding-window attention exist in modern systems.

04Part I · Foundations

Sequence-to-Sequence Architecture

Transformers didn't invent the idea of mapping one sequence to another — they inherited it from "seq2seq" models, and understanding that inheritance makes the Transformer's encoder-decoder split feel inevitable rather than arbitrary.

The task: sequence in, sequence out

Machine translation is the canonical example: input "I love machine learning" (English, 4 tokens) must become output "J'aime l'apprentissage automatique" (French, 4 tokens) — a different length, a different vocabulary, a different word order. Summarization, speech-to-text, and question answering share this same shape: variable-length input, variable-length output.

The original (2014) seq2seq recipe, with RNNs

Encoder RNN reads the input sentence, one token at a timefinal hidden state = a single "context vector" summarizing the whole input
Decoder RNN generates the output sentence, one token at a timeconditioned on that one context vector, plus its own previous outputs
The bottleneck that mattered

Everything about a 50-word input sentence had to be squeezed into one fixed-size vector before the decoder ever saw it. For short sentences this worked surprisingly well; for long ones, quality dropped off sharply, because early words in a long sentence were effectively "forgotten" by the time the context vector was formed.

The fix that researchers found — letting the decoder look back at all of the encoder's hidden states, not just the final one, and learn how much to weight each one — was exactly the attention mechanism (Bahdanau et al., 2014/2015). The Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) took this idea and made it the entire architecture, replacing recurrence altogether. That's the direct line from seq2seq → attention → Transformer.

What the Transformer keeps, and what it throws away

Not every Transformer keeps the encoder-decoder split at all — Section 05 covers the full split, and Sections 28–30 cover architectures that use only one half.

06Part II · Representing Text

Tokenization

Before any math happens, a sentence has to become a list of small, countable units. That process is tokenization, and the choices made here quietly shape everything downstream — vocabulary size, model size, even how well a model handles typos and rare words.

Intuition

A neural network can't read letters or words directly — it needs numbers. Tokenization is the "cutting board" step: chop the sentence into pieces, then look each piece up in a fixed list (the vocabulary) to get a number.

A simple example

The sentence "The cat is sleeping." splits into tokens, and each token maps to an ID from the model's vocabulary:

The cat is sleeping .
101 2054 2003 5777 1012

These specific IDs are illustrative examples, not necessarily a real tokenizer's actual output — every model has its own vocabulary and its own numbering.

Why subwords, not whole words?

Early NLP systems tokenized by whole word. That has two problems: the vocabulary needed to cover every word in a language is huge (hundreds of thousands of entries, and you'll still miss rare words, typos, and names), and it can't handle a word it has never seen at all — it hits an "unknown token" dead end. Subword tokenization fixes both by allowing common words to stay whole while rare or unseen words get split into smaller, previously-seen pieces.

Subword tokenization, in one line each
BPE
Byte-Pair Encoding. Starts from individual characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, building up a vocabulary of common substrings. Used by GPT-style models.
WordPiece
Similar merging idea to BPE, but picks merges that maximize the likelihood of the training data rather than raw frequency. Used by BERT. Continuation pieces are often marked with ##.
SentencePiece
A tokenizer framework (often paired with BPE or a unigram language model underneath) that treats the input as a raw stream of characters — including spaces — so it needs no language-specific pre-tokenization. Used by T5, Llama, and many multilingual models.
Why it matters
All three exist to solve the same problem: keep the vocabulary small and fixed, while still being able to represent any input string, however rare or novel, as a sequence of known pieces.

07Part II · Representing Text

Embeddings

A token ID is just an arbitrary integer — and arbitrary integers are dangerous to feed a neural network directly.

Why token IDs alone don't work

Suppose "cat" is ID 2054 and "dog" is ID 9182. Nothing about those two numbers tells the model the words are semantically related — and worse, a network might wrongly learn that ID 9182 is "bigger" or "later" than 2054 in some meaningful way, when the numbering is really just an arbitrary index into a lookup list. We need a representation where closeness in meaning corresponds to closeness in the representation itself.

The fix: embeddings

Token IDe.g. 2054
Embedding lookuprow in a big table
Vectore.g. [0.21, -0.73, 0.44, …]

An embedding is simply a row of numbers assigned to each token ID. Instead of one integer, "cat" becomes a vector like [0.21, -0.73, 0.44, …] with hundreds of values. These numbers are not hand-designed — they are learned parameters, adjusted during training so that words used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors.

The embedding matrix

All of these vectors live together in one big table: the embedding matrix. Its shape is:

Embedding matrix shape = Vocabulary Size × Embedding Dimension

Example: 50,000 × 768

Read that as: 50,000 rows (one per possible token in the vocabulary) and 768 columns (the size of the vector used to represent each one — commonly written d_model). Looking up a token's embedding is literally just reading one row out of this 50,000 × 768 table. That table itself is one of the largest groups of learned parameters in the whole model: 50,000 × 768 ≈ 38.4 million numbers, all trained.

Key terms
Vocabulary size
How many distinct tokens the model can represent — the number of rows in the embedding matrix. Typical range: 30,000–250,000.
Embedding dimension
How many numbers represent each token — the number of columns, often called d_model. Typical range: 256 (small models) to 12,000+ (very large models).
Learned embeddings
The embedding matrix starts out random and is updated by gradient descent along with every other weight in the model — it is not fixed or hand-built.
Common confusion

Token ID vs. embedding: a token ID is a single integer used purely as an array index. An embedding is the dense vector of learned numbers stored at that index. The ID itself never enters the math of the network — only the embedding it points to does.

08Part II · Representing Text

Positional Encoding

Transformers look at every token simultaneously — which is exactly what makes them fast (Section 02), and exactly why they need extra help to know what order the tokens came in.

The problem, precisely

Self-attention (Section 10) treats its input as a set, not a sequence — mathematically, if you shuffled the input tokens and shuffled the output the same way, attention alone wouldn't notice. But "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" use the exact same words. Without extra information, a Transformer literally cannot tell them apart. Positional encoding injects order information directly into each token's representation before it enters the first layer.

The original idea: sinusoidal positional encoding

The 2017 paper's solution: generate a fixed vector for every position using sine and cosine waves of different frequencies, and add it element-wise to the token embedding.

PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos / 100002i / d_model )
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 100002i / d_model )
pos
the position of the token in the sequence (0, 1, 2, …)
i
an index over pairs of dimensions — dimension 0,1 form pair i=0; dimensions 2,3 form pair i=1; and so on
d_model
the total embedding dimension (e.g. 512) — the length of the positional encoding vector matches it exactly, so it can be added to the token embedding
10000
a fixed constant chosen so wavelengths range from short (2π) to long (about 10000·2π) across the dimensions

Why sine and cosine, and why different frequencies?

  • Different frequencies per dimension pair mean each position gets a unique combination of values — like a fingerprint made of clock hands ticking at different speeds. Low dimension indices oscillate quickly (encode fine-grained position); high dimension indices oscillate slowly (encode coarse, long-range position).
  • Sine and cosine together (rather than sine alone) give each frequency a two-dimensional "clock hand" (an (x,y) point on a circle), which turns out to make relative positions expressible as a simple linear transformation: PE(pos+k) can be written as a fixed rotation applied to PE(pos), for any fixed offset k. That means the model can learn to attend by relative distance, not just absolute position — a very convenient mathematical property, without requiring extra learned parameters for it.
  • Because it's a fixed formula rather than a learned table, positions beyond what was seen in training can still be computed — in principle giving some ability to generalize to longer sequences than were trained on (in practice, this extrapolation is limited, which is part of why modern models often use other schemes).

Beyond the original paper: what modern models actually use

Instead of a fixed sinusoidal formula, treat position like another token: build a second embedding table of shape max_length × d_model, one learnable row per position, and add the row for each token's position to its token embedding. Simpler to implement, but it can't generalize past whatever max_length was set during training. Used by the original BERT and GPT-2.

Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE), used in most modern LLMs (Llama, GPT-NeoX-style models, and many others), takes a different approach entirely: rather than adding a positional vector to the embedding, it rotates the Query and Key vectors (Section 11) by an angle that depends on their position, before computing attention scores. Because a dot product between two rotated vectors depends mathematically only on the difference in their rotation angles, this bakes relative position directly into the attention score itself — and tends to generalize better to sequence lengths longer than those seen in training than either of the methods above.

ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases) skips modifying the vectors at all, and instead directly subtracts a penalty from the raw attention scores that grows with the distance between two tokens — closer tokens get less penalty, distant tokens get more. It's simple and known for good length extrapolation, and has been used in some open models.

Accuracy note

The original 2017 Transformer used only the fixed sinusoidal formula above. RoPE, ALiBi, and learned positional embeddings used in other models are all later developments by different research teams — none of them were part of the original paper. Always check which scheme a specific model actually uses before assuming.

09Part III · Attention

Attention

Attention answers one question, asked separately for every single token: "Of everything else in this sequence, how much should I pay attention to each piece, and why?"

🔦
Analogy. Imagine each word carries a small flashlight. When the model processes a word, that word "shines its light" on every other word in the sentence, brighter on the ones that matter to understanding it, dimmer on the ones that don't. Attention is the mechanism that decides how bright each beam should be.

The classic example

Take: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired." The word "it" is ambiguous in isolation — grammatically it could refer to "animal" or "street." But semantically, only an animal can be "tired," so a model with good attention should link "it" strongly to "animal," weakly to "street."

Attention, made visible

Click any word to see where it "looks." Weights below are illustrative — hand-set to build intuition here. From Section 12 onward, you'll compute real ones from real (if tiny) matrices.

What "attention" means, precisely

Attention = "how much should one token pay attention to another token?"

Mechanically, attention takes one token's query (what it's looking for) and compares it against every token's key (what it offers), producing a relevance score per pair. Those scores are turned into weights that sum to 1 (via softmax), and used to blend together every token's value (what it actually contributes) into a new, context-aware representation. Section 11 unpacks Query, Key, and Value one at a time — this section is only asking you to trust that "relevance scores → weighted blend" is the right idea before the matrices show up.

Attention is more general than "self-attention"

The word "attention" describes the mechanism in general. Whether the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence (self-attention, Section 10) or from two different sequences (cross-attention, Section 15) is a separate design choice layered on top of the same underlying computation.

Self-Attention

"Self"-attention just means: the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence. Every token compares itself against every token in its own sentence — including itself.

Why this matters

Because every row is computed independently but references the entire sequence, self-attention gives each token a direct, learnable way to gather exactly the context it needs — a pronoun can pull in its referent, a verb can pull in its subject, a closing quotation mark can pull in its opening one — regardless of how far away that information sits.

Common confusion

Attention vs. self-attention: attention is the general mechanism (compare queries against keys, blend values). Self-attention is the specific, common case where the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence. Cross-attention (Section 15) is the other case, where they come from two different sequences.

Query, Key, Value

Q, K, and V are the three roles every token plays simultaneously in attention. Get the analogy solid before touching the matrices.

The analogy

Query — "What am I looking for?"

Think of it as the question a token brings to the table. When processing the word "it," its query roughly encodes "I'm a pronoun — I need to find whatever noun I refer to."

Key — "What information do I contain?"

Every token also advertises what it has to offer, so others can decide whether it's relevant to them. "animal"'s key might roughly encode "I'm a living, tireable subject."

Value — "What information should I provide?"

Once relevance is decided, this is the actual content that gets passed along. Keys are like a label on a filing cabinet drawer; values are what's actually inside the drawer.

📚
Library analogy. A query is your search term at the reference desk. Keys are the index cards / catalog tags on every book. Value is the actual content of the book you end up reading. You compare your search term against catalog tags (query · key) to decide which books are relevant, then read (weighted-average, in this case) their actual contents (value).

Why divide by √dk?

As the key dimension dk grows, dot products between random-ish vectors tend to grow larger in magnitude too (more terms being summed). Very large scores push softmax into a regime where it becomes extremely "peaky" — one weight near 1, all others near 0 — which in turn makes gradients during training tiny almost everywhere. Dividing by √dk rescales the scores back to a sane range before softmax, keeping gradients healthy. It's a normalization trick, not a conceptual necessity — but in practice, it matters a lot for stable training.

Multi-Head Attention

One attention computation forces every token to compress "what's relevant to me" into a single blend. Multi-head attention runs several of these in parallel, each with its own learned projections, so the model can track several kinds of relationships at once.

🎯
Analogy. Reading a sentence for grammar, for tone, and for factual claims at the same time takes three different kinds of attention. Multi-head attention gives the model several independent "attention specialists" per layer, each looking at the same input through its own learned lens, rather than forcing one specialist to do everything.
A careful claim, not an overclaim

Some heads do turn out to specialize in interpretable ways after training (e.g., one head that consistently tracks subject-verb agreement). But this isn't guaranteed or universal — many heads learn patterns that don't map cleanly onto any single human-nameable role, and which pattern ends up where can vary by training run. Treat "different heads learn different relationships" as a tendency the architecture enables, not a guarantee every head fulfills.

14Part III · Attention

Masked Self-Attention

Critical for GPT-style generation: a token being predicted must not be able to see the future. Masked self-attention enforces that rule directly inside the attention computation.

The rule

  • Token 1 → can see Token 1
  • Token 2 → can see Token 1, Token 2
  • Token 3 → can see Token 1, Token 2, Token 3

But never the reverse — Token 1 cannot see Token 2 or Token 3, and Token 2 cannot see Token 3. This is called a causal constraint: each position can only be influenced by itself and whatever came before it.

Why future tokens must be hidden

During autoregressive generation (Section 23), the model produces one token at a time — token 5 genuinely does not exist yet when the model is deciding token 3. If training let the model "peek" at token 5 while learning to predict token 3, it would learn a shortcut that's simply unavailable at real generation time, and the model would fail the moment it had to generate for real. Masking during training exactly recreates the information constraint the model will face during inference — see Section 26 (Teacher Forcing) for how this lets training still happen in parallel despite the sequential generation rule.

How masking is actually implemented

Before the softmax step of scaled dot-product attention, every "not allowed to see" position in the raw score matrix is overwritten with a very large negative number (conceptually −∞). Softmax then turns those positions into a weight of essentially 0 — the value vectors at those positions simply don't contribute, without needing any special-case code in the weighted-sum step.

Expanding to a wider hidden dimension (commonly 4× d_model) before the non-linearity gives the network more room to represent complex functions of each token, then compresses back down so the block's output can be added residually (Section 17) to its input, which requires matching shapes. Layer Normalization

Layer normalization keeps the numbers flowing through the network in a well-behaved range, which makes training faster and more stable.

The formula

LayerNorm(x) = γ · x − μσ + β
μ
the mean of x's values, computed across the features of a single token (not across the batch, and not across other tokens)
σ
the standard deviation of x's values, same scope as μ
γ, β
learned scale and shift parameters — one pair per feature dimension — that let the network undo the normalization if that's actually better for a given dimension

In words: for one token's vector, subtract its own mean and divide by its own standard deviation (so its values now average 0 with unit variance), then apply a learned rescale and shift. This is done independently for every token, at every position, at every layer.

Why normalize at all?

As data passes through many layers, the scale of the numbers can drift — growing or shrinking in ways that make training unstable (similar in spirit to the vanishing/exploding gradient problem from Section 03). Renormalizing at each layer keeps values in a predictable range regardless of what came before, which in practice lets you train deeper networks, with larger learning rates, more reliably.

Don't confuse layer normalization with batch normalization. Batch norm normalizes across the batch dimension (using statistics shared across many examples); layer norm normalizes across the feature dimension of one single token, independent of everything else in the batch — which matters a lot for variable-length sequences and small batch sizes, both common in NLP.

Inference / Generation Flow

Generation looks completely different from training, even though it's the exact same trained weights doing the work.

Autoregressive generation

At inference time there is no target sentence to shift right — the model has to invent the output one token at a time, feeding each new token back in as input for the next step:

  1. Input: "The sky is" → model predicts a probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next token → picks "blue"
  2. New input: "The sky is blue" → predicts the next token → picks "today"
  3. New input: "The sky is blue today" → predicts the next token → picks "."
  4. A special end-of-sequence token is predicted (or a length limit is reached) → generation stops

Loss Function

Training needs one number to measure "how wrong was the prediction?" For next-token prediction, that number is cross-entropy loss.

Intuition first

At every position, the model outputs a full probability distribution over the vocabulary (Section 12's softmax). We know the actual correct next word. Cross-entropy loss asks a simple question: how much probability did the model assign to the word that was actually correct? High probability on the right answer → low loss. Low probability on the right answer → high loss, and steeply so.

L = − Σi yi log(pi)
yi
the true label, as a "one-hot" vector — 1 for the correct word, 0 for every other word in the vocabulary
pi
the model's predicted probability for word i
log
natural logarithm

Because yi is 0 for every word except the correct one, every term in that sum vanishes except one — the formula collapses to simply L = −log(p_correct): the negative log of the probability the model assigned to the actually-correct word.

Why the logarithm?

−log(p) is 0 when p = 1 (perfect confidence, no penalty) and grows without bound as p → 0 (confidently wrong gets punished severely — much more severely than a linear penalty would). This steep curve near p=0 is precisely what pushes the model to avoid being confidently wrong, not just wrong.

25Part V · Training & Inference

Backpropagation

This isn't Transformer-specific — it's how essentially every deep neural network learns — but it's worth being precise about, since it's the mechanism that turns "loss" into "updated weights."

Intuition

The network is a long chain of mathematical operations: embedding lookup → attention → FFN → attention → FFN → … → softmax → loss. Backpropagation asks, for every single weight in that chain: "if I nudged this one weight slightly, how much would the final loss change?" That sensitivity is called a gradient. Once you know every weight's gradient, you nudge each weight a small step in the direction that reduces the loss. That's gradient descent.

The mechanism: the chain rule

∂L/∂w = ∂L/∂out · ∂out/∂w

The gradient of the loss with respect to an early weight is the product of gradients along every step between that weight and the final loss — the chain rule from calculus, applied automatically, layer by layer, backward through the network.

In practice, this is handled automatically by "automatic differentiation" in modern deep learning frameworks — you never hand-derive these formulas for a real model. What's worth internalizing is why depth makes this hard: the gradient for an early layer is a product of many terms, one per layer between it and the loss. If those terms tend to be small, the product shrinks toward zero (vanishing gradients, Section 03); if they tend to be large, it explodes.

How the Transformer's own design helps here

  • Residual connections (Section 17) give gradients a direct "+x" path backward through every sublayer, independent of how small the sublayer's own gradient might be.
  • Layer normalization (Section 18) keeps activations — and therefore gradients — in a stable numerical range at every layer.

Together, these are a large part of why Transformers can be stacked dozens (even hundreds) of layers deep and still train reliably — something that was much harder for un-aided deep recurrent networks.

26Part V · Training & Inference

Teacher Forcing

A training trick that makes parallel training of an inherently sequential (autoregressive) task possible.

🎹
Analogy. Learning piano by playing along with a recording, where every note you're about to play is shown to you regardless of whether you got the last note right, versus having to build each new note entirely on top of your own possibly-wrong previous note. Teacher forcing is the first approach — the model always gets the real "next correct input," never has to recover from its own mistake mid-sequence during training.

What it means concretely

When training the decoder, instead of feeding it its own (partially trained, likely wrong early on) previous predictions, you feed it the real target sequence, shifted right by one position — so that when predicting position i, it has seen the true tokens at positions 1..i−1, not its own guesses.

What problem it solves

  • Enables parallel training. Because every position's decoder input is already known in advance (it's just the real target sequence, shifted), all positions can be computed in one parallel forward pass — you don't have to wait for position 3's prediction before feeding position 4, the way you would if you used the model's own outputs.
  • Faster, more stable early training. Early in training, a model's own predictions are close to random. Without teacher forcing, one bad early guess would derail every subsequent position in that sequence, making early training signal very noisy.

The trade-off: exposure bias

Because the model always trains on real, correct prefixes but must generate from its own, possibly imperfect, prefixes at inference time (Section 23), there's a train/inference mismatch called exposure bias — errors that would never occur during training can compound during real generation. This is a known, studied limitation, not a bug that's been fully "solved"; various techniques (e.g. scheduled sampling, which occasionally trains on the model's own predictions instead) have been explored as partial mitigations.

27Part V · Training & Inference

Causal Mask — the training/inference connection

Section 14 covered how the causal mask works, inside attention. This section covers why it's non-negotiable, once you understand teacher forcing.

The subtle question worth sitting with

If teacher forcing already hands the decoder the entire real target sequence at once, what stops the model from simply "cheating" — peeking at position i+1's real token to help predict position i, since it's sitting right there in the input?

The causal mask is precisely what stops this. Even though the full target sequence is present in the decoder's input tensor during training (thanks to teacher forcing), the mask prevents attention at position i from ever looking at positions after i. Without it, the model would learn a trivial shortcut — "just copy the next token" — that produces a near-zero training loss but is completely useless, because at real inference time (Section 23) there is no future token to copy; it hasn't been generated yet.

Put together, teacher forcing and the causal mask solve two different halves of the same puzzle: teacher forcing makes parallel training possible; the causal mask makes sure that parallelism doesn't let the model learn a shortcut that only works when the answer is already sitting in front of it.

One-line summary

Teacher forcing: "train on the real sequence, all at once, for speed." Causal masking: "...but never let a position see further ahead than it's allowed to at real generation time." You need both together — one without the other either breaks parallelism or breaks correctness.

28Part VI · Model Families

Encoder-only Models

Keep only the encoder half of the architecture — no decoder, no cross-attention, no causal mask.

Every token attends bidirectionally to every other token, so the model builds the richest possible in-context representation of the whole input — but it has no built-in mechanism for generating new text one token at a time. That makes encoder-only models a strong fit for "understanding" tasks: classification, extracting spans, scoring similarity — anything where the output is a label, a score, or a selection from the input, rather than freely generated new text.

The flagship example is BERT — full details in Section 31.

29Part VI · Model Families

Decoder-only Models

Keep only the decoder half — but drop the cross-attention sublayer too, since there's no separate encoder output to attend to. What's left is masked self-attention plus feed-forward, stacked N times.

Every token can only see itself and earlier tokens (the causal mask, Section 14), which is exactly the constraint needed for autoregressive generation: predict the next token, append it, repeat. This makes decoder-only models a natural fit for open-ended text generation — and, with a large enough model and enough data, for a surprising range of other tasks too, simply by phrasing them as "predict the next token" (this is the basis of modern general-purpose LLMs).

The flagship example is GPT — full details in Section 32.

Encoder-Decoder Models

Keep both halves — the full original architecture from Section 21, cross-attention included.

This shape is the most natural fit for tasks that are explicitly "transform one sequence into a different sequence": translation, summarization, and — in a particularly flexible design choice — any NLP task at all, by framing it as text-to-text (input a task description plus input text, output the answer as text). Why bidirectionality is BERT's whole point

For understanding a sentence you already fully have (e.g. classifying its sentiment), there's no reason to artificially restrict a token to only see earlier words — seeing the whole sentence at once, in both directions, gives a richer representation. BERT's masked-language-modeling objective is specifically designed to exploit that, since it would be trivial (and useless) to predict a masked word using a causal mask that only reveals words before it.

KV Cache — why autoregressive inference doesn't recompute everything

During generation (Section 23), producing token N requires attention over tokens 1..N. Without any optimization, that means recomputing Key and Value vectors for every previous token, at every generation step — wasteful, since those tokens' Keys and Values never change once computed (only the newest token's Q, K, V are new).

Without KV cache

At every new step, recompute K and V for the entire sequence so far, from scratch, at every layer.

Step 5: recompute K,V for tokens 1,2,3,4,5
Step 6: recompute K,V for tokens 1,2,3,4,5,6
… quadratic total work.

With KV cache

Store each token's K and V the first time they're computed. At each new step, compute K and V only for the single newest token, and reuse the cached ones for everything before it.

Step 5: reuse K,V for 1–4, compute only for 5
Step 6: reuse K,V for 1–5, compute only for 6
… linear total work.

This is a pure inference-time engineering optimization — it changes nothing about the model's outputs, only how efficiently they're computed. It does cost memory (the cache grows with sequence length × number of layers × number of heads), which is exactly the cost that Grouped-Query Attention, above, was designed to reduce.

36Part VII · Scaling Up

Computational Complexity

Self-attention's biggest structural cost, stated plainly: it's quadratic in sequence length.

Self-attention compute & memory ~ O(n²·d)

n = sequence length, d = model dimension

Why quadratic, intuitively

Attention computes a relevance score between every pair of tokens — that's what QKᵀ (Section 12) actually is: an n × n matrix. Double the sequence length, and the number of pairs doesn't double — it roughly quadruples, because you now have twice as many tokens each comparing against twice as many others.

Watch the pairwise count explode

Memory implications

That n × n attention matrix has to be held in memory (at least transiently) for every attention head, at every layer. For n = 1,000 tokens that's a million entries per head; for n = 100,000 tokens (a long document or a big codebase), it's ten billion entries per head — which is precisely why long-context models need real engineering (sparse attention, sliding windows, chunking, or fundamentally different mechanisms) rather than "just running the same formula on more tokens."

Contrast with Section 03

Recall the trade-off from the RNN comparison: RNNs cost O(n) per step but pay for it with O(n) path length between distant tokens and no parallelism. Transformers flip that trade: O(1) path length and full parallelism, paid for with O(n²) cost. Neither is free — which one wins depends on your sequence lengths and hardware.

37Part VIII · Review

Common Mistakes & Confusions

Eleven pairs of terms that get mixed up constantly — in casual conversation, in blog posts, and in interviews. Click each to expand.

Attention is the general mechanism: compare a query against keys, blend values by the resulting weights. Self-attention is the specific case where the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence. Every self-attention computation is an attention computation; not every attention computation is self-attention.
Self-attention: Q, K, V all from the same sequence. Cross-attention: Q from one sequence (the decoder), K and V from a different one (the encoder's output). Same formula, different sources.
Encoder: reads the whole input at once, bidirectional self-attention, no causal mask — built for understanding. Decoder: generates output one token at a time, causal self-attention (plus cross-attention if paired with an encoder) — built for generation.
Embedding: a learned vector representing what a token is (its identity/meaning). Positional encoding: a vector (learned or fixed) representing where a token sits in the sequence. They're the same shape and get added together, but encode completely different information.
Token ID: a single integer, just an index into a lookup table — carries no semantic meaning by itself. Embedding: the dense vector of learned numbers stored at that index — this is what actually enters the network's math.
Query: what this token is looking for. Key: what a token advertises about itself, for others to match against. Value: the actual content a token contributes once it's deemed relevant. All three are different linear projections of the same input vector.
Logits: raw, unbounded scores straight out of a linear layer — can be any real number, don't sum to anything meaningful. Probabilities: what you get after applying softmax to logits — non-negative, sum to 1, interpretable as confidence.
Training: the decoder sees the real target sequence at once (teacher forcing), all positions computed in parallel, weights get updated. Inference: the decoder sees only its own previously generated tokens, strictly one step at a time, weights are frozen.
BERT: encoder-only, bidirectional, trained to fill in masked words, not built for free generation. GPT: decoder-only, causal, trained to predict the next word, built specifically for generation.
"Transformer" names an architecture (Section 21). "LLM" (Large Language Model) names a category of model defined by scale and training approach. Nearly all modern LLMs use a (heavily modified) Transformer architecture, but "Transformer" and "LLM" aren't synonyms — you could build a small Transformer that's not an LLM, and researchers do explore non-Transformer architectures for large language modeling too.
Model parameters (WQ, WK, WV, WO, FFN weights, …) are learned once during training and then fixed. Attention weights (the softmax output in Section 12) are computed fresh for every single input, every single forward pass — they're an output of the computation, not a stored, learned quantity themselves.

Quick Revision

Transformer in 60 seconds

Text is split into tokens, each mapped to a learned vector (embedding), with position information added since the model reads everything at once. Inside the model, self-attention lets every token gather relevant context from every other token by comparing a query against everyone's key and blending their values — done several times in parallel (multi-head attention). A per-token feed-forward network then adds further transformation. Residual connections and layer normalization wrap around both, keeping training stable across many stacked layers. An encoder does this bidirectionally over the input; a decoder does it causally (masked) over the output-so-far, and — if paired with an encoder — also cross-attends into it. The final layer turns the last vector into a probability distribution over the vocabulary, via linear + softmax, and the model is trained by comparing that distribution to the real next token with cross-entropy loss, adjusting every weight via backpropagation. At inference time, this happens one token at a time, autoregressively, each new token feeding back in as input for the next.

Transformer in 10 steps

  1. Tokenize — split raw text into tokens against a fixed vocabulary.
  2. Convert tokens to embeddings — look up each token's learned vector.
  3. Add positional information — inject order, since attention alone is order-blind.
  4. Create Q, K, V — project each token's vector three ways with learned matrices.
  5. Calculate attention — softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)·V, per token, over the whole (allowed) sequence.
  6. Multi-head attention — run several attention computations in parallel, then concatenate and project.
  7. Feed-forward transformation — a per-token, two-layer non-linear transformation.
  8. Residual + normalization — add each sublayer's input back to its output, then normalize.
  9. Produce a contextual representation — repeat steps 4–8 across N stacked layers.
  10. Predict output / next token — project to vocabulary size, softmax, pick (or sample) a token.
